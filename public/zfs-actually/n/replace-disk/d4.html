A disk just died 2/4 ← prev next →

A disk has died. The instinct is either panicked speed or frozen paralysis. Actually, a pool that lost a disk is stable and still serving—the procedure is unhurried, and the danger that actually bites people is imprecision: pulling the wrong disk out of a machine full of identical-looking drives. The command itself is one line: zpool replace "is equivalent to attaching new-device, waiting for it to resilver, and then detaching device" (zpool-replace(8)). Everything around that one line is about being absolutely sure which physical object you're touching.

⚠ DESTRUCTIVE—verify before running: zpool replace wipes and takes over the new disk, and pulling the wrong disk from a pool that's already down one can kill the pool. Semantics per zpool-replace(8).

The procedure:

  1. Identify beyond doubt. Map the failed device to a physical serial number (zpool status names the device; smartctl or /dev/disk/by-id gives the serial printed on the label). This step is the whole game—this is why pools should be built with by-id paths, not /dev/sdX names that reshuffle across reboots.
  2. Swap the hardware (hot-swap bays or a powered-off swap—either is fine; the pool imports degraded and waits).
  3. zpool replace tank <old-device> <new-device>—the new disk must be at least "the minimum size of all the devices" in the vdev (zpool-replace(8)). Resilver begins immediately; the pool serves throughout.
  4. Watch it finish (zpool status), then scrub to certify the whole pool. Note: starting the replace "cancels any in-progress scrub" (man page)—resilver first, scrub after, not the reverse.

Judgment calls around the mechanics:

  • Failing vs failed—a disk that still limps (rising errors, not yet dead) can be replaced while alive: zpool replace with both old and new connected reads from the failing disk too, keeping full redundancy through the resilver. Strictly better when you have the ports—don't pull the sick disk first if you don't have to.
  • The window—the resilver is your thinnest moment (see Resilvering); a RAIDZ1 rebuild across correlated, same-batch survivors is where healthy paranoia earns its keep. Have the replacement on the shelf before the failure; the window starts when the disk dies, not when the courier arrives.
  • Spares—a spare vdev automates step 2 for machines you don't watch daily; for a watched homelab, the shelf spare plus this procedure is usually enough.
  • After it's boring—the first replacement teaches you your bay labeling, your by-id discipline, and your nerves. Do the serial-number mapping now, on a healthy pool, and tape the printout to the case.
