ZFS, Actually Storage you can finally reason about.

Checksums

concept
depth 4/4 − deeper + surface

The naive model: "RAID protects my data, and corruption announces itself as errors." Actually, disks lie silently—they return wrong bits while reporting success—and traditional RAID can't even ask which of its copies is right. ZFS checksums every block and verifies on every read, so wrong data cannot travel: it's caught the moment anything touches it.

Hold the two powers apart, because everything downstream depends on the split: checksums detect, unconditionally. Repair requires a second good copy—a mirror, RAIDZ parity. Detection without redundancy tells you what you lost; it can't give it back.

Where you meet them: zpool status has three error columns—READ, WRITE, CKSUM. A rising CKSUM count means a device returned data that failed verification: silent corruption, caught in the act. With redundancy present, ZFS already repaired it from the good copy ("For replicated (mirror, raidz, or draid) devices, ZFS automatically repairs any damage discovered"—zpool-scrub(8)) and the count is your notification. Persistent CKSUM errors on one disk mean a dying disk or a bad cable—SATA cables are the classic false villain, cheap to swap first.

A scrub is this same verification, forced over everything, including cold data nothing has read in years—so corruption gets found while the redundancy to fix it still exists.

What this replaces and what it neighbors:

  • Traditional RAID mirrors bytes, not truth. Feed it silent corruption and it mirrors the corruption faithfully; ask it which side of a disagreement is right and the interface has no such question. Checksums are the missing arbiter—this is what "self-healing" actually is: detection (checksum) plus a decidable repair (the copy that verifies).
  • Bit rot is the phenomenon checksums exist for—media degrading in place, cosmic-ray flips, firmware lies. Rare per-block, near-certain at pool-scale over years.
  • The ECC question—checksums verify the disk's honesty; RAM sits above them. The panic version of that observation is mostly myth, but the layering is real (see The ECC Question).

The structural trick is where checksums live: not beside the data (a disk that corrupts a block would corrupt its adjacent checksum just as happily) but in the parent block pointer. Every pointer carries the checksum of what it points to, so verification walks downward: trust the parent, check the child, descend. Each pool-wide commit produces a new root, so the whole pool is a Merkle tree—the root (uberblock) transitively vouches for every byte below it.

This is the same construction as Git and blockchains, deployed for the same reason: tamper-evidence, where the tamperer is physics. It also makes the checksum self-verifying in context—a corrupted checksum fails one level up, so there's no chicken-and-egg. Default algorithm fletcher4 (fast, error-detecting); sha256 when cryptographic strength is wanted (dedup). Overhead: single-digit CPU percent, unconditional—ZFS considers reading without verifying to be a category error, and after your first caught CKSUM error, so will you.
