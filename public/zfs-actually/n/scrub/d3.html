Scrubs

The word is active—you scrub a thing to clean it—so the naive model makes scrub the repair process. Actually, a scrub is a forced read: "a normal scrub examines all data in the specified pools and verifies each block's checksum" (zpool-scrub(8)). Detection was always going to happen on read; the scrub's contribution is reading the data nothing else reads, so cold corruption is found while the redundancy to fix it still exists.

Repair happens only where a second good copy lives: "for replicated (mirror, raidz, or draid) devices, ZFS automatically repairs any damage discovered." Scrub a redundancy-less vdev and ZFS hands you a list of what rotted—named files, even—and can heal none of it.

The practice: zpool scrub tank, online, background, throttled behind real traffic—expect hours to a day-plus on big HDD pools. Monthly is the homelab convention, usually via a systemd timer or cron a distro already installed (check before adding a second one). Then read the results in zpool status: repaired 0B ... with 0 errors is the sentence you want. A scrub that repairs a few blocks did its job; a device whose CKSUM count rises scrub after scrub is announcing its retirement—that, plus a rising trend, is your early-warning system working.

Why it must be scheduled at all: your hot data self-verifies on every read. The archive nobody has opened in three years verifies never—unless something walks it. The scrub is that something.
