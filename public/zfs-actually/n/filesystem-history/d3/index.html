Start from zero: what a filesystem is, and why ZFS exists 2/4 ← prev next →

Filesystem history looks like a parade of product names—FAT, ext2, NTFS, ZFS. Actually, it's a chain of disasters and answers. Three generations, each built to survive the failure that ruined the one before:

  1. Simple ledgers (1970s–80s: FAT, early Unix filesystems). Fast and tiny. But lose power mid-write and the ledger itself ends up half-updated—scrambled records, orphaned files, a repair tool run at every boot, and sometimes real loss.
  2. Journaling (1990s: NTFS, ext3, XFS). Before touching the ledger, write a short note—a journal entry—saying what you're about to do. After a crash, replay the notes. The ledger stays honest. This generation still runs most of the world.
  3. Integrity (2000s onward: ZFS first at full scale, then Btrfs and Apple's APFS). Journaling protects the ledger from crashes—but it still trusts whatever the disk returns. This generation assumes the hardware itself can lie, and verifies everything.

You can date any filesystem you meet by which disaster it answers:

  • FAT32 / exFAT (generation 1) still rule USB sticks and camera cards—not out of nostalgia, but because when every device must read every stick, simplicity is the feature.
  • ext4, XFS, NTFS (generation 2) are the workhorses under most Linux and Windows machines today: journaled, fast, thoroughly debugged, and still fundamentally trusting of the disk.
  • ZFS, Btrfs, APFS (generation 3) each took the new assumptions somewhere different: ZFS to servers and many disks, APFS to the SSD in your Mac, Btrfs into the mainline Linux kernel.

The practical takeaway is calm: the older generations aren't wrong or obsolete—each survives exactly where its trade-offs fit. But when a machine holds years of data that must stay verifiably intact, generation 3 is the one built for that job.
