ZFS is a system for storing data, built around one promise: what you put in comes back exactly as you wrote it—or ZFS tells you, loudly, instead of silently handing you something broken.

In practice, it does four things for you:

  • Gathers any number of disks into one pool of storage, so you stop thinking about individual drives.
  • Checks everything it reads for damage, and repairs damage automatically from a spare copy.
  • Freezes moments in time (snapshots)—instantly, at no cost—so you can rewind a mistake.
  • Copies those moments to another machine, efficiently, which is what a real backup is.

People reach for ZFS when a computer's job is to hold data that matters: years of photos, a business's records, a home server. It's usually called a filesystem, and it is one—but that undersells it, in a way that becomes interesting once the basics are solid (that story is a level deeper).
