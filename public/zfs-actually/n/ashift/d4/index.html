Ashift

concept
depth 3/3 − deeper + surface

ZFS trains you to expect mutability—nearly everything is a property you can change anytime. Ashift is the exception: the sector size a vdev assumes, baked into its on-disk format at creation—"it is set at vdev creation and it is immutable" (OpenZFS docs). It's a power of two: ashift=9 means 512-byte sectors, ashift=12 means 4K.

That's why the guides scream about one small number: it's the single decision in ZFS that is actually a foundation pour.

The failure mode: modern drives have 4K physical sectors, but many report 512 for compatibility. Trust the lie and you get ashift=9 on 4K hardware—and every sub-4K write becomes a read-modify-write cycle inside the drive, forever, invisibly, on every vdev member. The pool works; it's just permanently slower, and the only exit is destroying and rebuilding the vdev.

The practice is blunt: zpool create -o ashift=12 unless you can prove your drives are truly 512-native (almost nothing you'd buy today is). ashift=12 on a genuinely-512 drive costs a little slack space; ashift=9 on a 4K drive costs performance permanently. The asymmetry does the deciding. Check an existing pool with zdb -C tank | grep ashift—better to know than to wonder.

Where it sits among its neighbors:

  • vs recordsize—the two "block size" ideas. Ashift is the physical floor per vdev, immutable; recordsize is the logical ceiling per dataset, changeable at will. When someone says "block size" about ZFS, make them say which.
  • Per vdev, not per pool—every vdev added over the pool's life makes its own ashift decision (modern ZFS detects 4K correctly most of the time, but "most of the time" is doing load-bearing work—pass the flag).
  • Why it can't change—an honest limitation, honestly derived: ashift shapes how every block pointer in the vdev addresses the disk. Changing it would mean rewriting the vdev's every allocation—which is just "rebuild the vdev" wearing a friendlier name. ZFS declines to pretend otherwise.

⚠ Destructive context—ashift is set by zpool create / zpool add / zpool attach, commands that repartition disks. Triple-check device names before running them; see Your First Pool.
