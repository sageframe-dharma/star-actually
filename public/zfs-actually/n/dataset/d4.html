Datasets

concept
depth 3/3 − deeper + surface
Build your first pool with intent 7/9 ← prev next →

A dataset is a filesystem carved out of the pool—it mounts, it holds files, it looks like a directory from the outside. The naive model says it's either a fancy folder or a small partition. Actually, a dataset is a policy boundary: it has no fixed size (all datasets share the pool's one reservoir), costs almost nothing to create, and is the unit at which everything interesting happens—properties, quotas, snapshots, replication.

The posture correction: on ext4 you'd make a directory; on ZFS you should usually make a dataset. Make many.

The rule of thumb: anything you might want to snapshot, replicate, quota, or tune separately gets its own dataset. A directory can't be snapshotted, sent to another machine, or given its own recordsize; a dataset can.

This is why a service-deployment setup makes a separate dataset per service rather than a folder per service: each service gets its own snapshot policy (the database hourly, the media library weekly), its own replication schedule, its own properties—and a restore of one service doesn't drag the others backward in time with it.

Datasets nest into a tree (tank/services/postgres), and children inherit properties from parents—so policy gets set once at the sensible level and flows down. Creating one is instant: zfs create tank/services/newthing.

The boundaries that actually matter:

  • Dataset vs directory—invisible day-to-day, decisive the day you need history: snapshots, sends, and rollbacks stop at dataset edges. The granularity you chose at creation is the granularity of your disaster recovery.
  • Dataset vs partition—no fixed size is the liberation, but it cuts both ways: nothing stops one dataset from eating the pool. quota (ceiling) and reservation (guaranteed floor) reintroduce limits as policy where you actually want them, instead of as geometry everywhere.
  • Dataset vs zvol—same carving, different face: a dataset presents a filesystem; a zvol presents a raw block device (what Proxmox gives VMs).

One warm-blooded warning: zfs destroy on a dataset takes its snapshots and its children with it, and there is no undo. The cheapness of creation does not extend to deletion.
