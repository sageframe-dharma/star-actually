ZIL and SLOG

concept
depth 4/4 − deeper + surface
Where the speed actually comes from 3/5 ← prev next →

The naive model—writes land on the fast SLOG first, then flush to the slow pool—is a write cache, and it's wrong in every part. Actually, the ZIL (ZFS Intent Log) is a crash log: written constantly, and in normal operation never read at all. It's read exactly once—at pool import after a crash, to replay acknowledged writes that hadn't reached their final home ("On system startup, ZFS reads the ZIL and replays any pending transactions"—Klara). Data does not flow through it; writes stage in RAM regardless.

A SLOG is nothing but the ZIL relocated to a fast dedicated device. Every pool already has a ZIL without one, living on the pool's own disks.

The part that decides whether you should care: only synchronous writes touch the ZIL. An async write (most writes—file copies, media, general homelab traffic) is acknowledged from RAM and flows to the pool in the next transaction group; "neither the ZIL nor the SLOG make a difference with pending asynchronous writes… if you don't have any applications that use synchronous writes, you don't need a SLOG" (Klara).

Sync writes—databases honoring fsync, NFS exports, VMs whose guests demand durability—must be on stable storage before ZFS acknowledges. Without a SLOG that means a hurried write to the ZIL area of your spinning pool per fsync; latency suffers. A SLOG (low-latency, power-loss-protected SSD—Optane-class ideally) absorbs exactly that. Check whether you even have the problem: zilstat, or watch pool latency while a sync-heavy workload runs.

Placed against its false siblings:

  • vs a write cache—RAM is the write cache (dirty data waiting in the transaction group). The ZIL is durability insurance on the acknowledgment, not a stop on the data path.
  • vs L2ARC—read-side spillover cache; loss is harmless. Losing a SLOG is harmless except if it dies together with a crash before replay—the one window where the log was about to be needed. Cheap consumer SSDs without power-loss protection quietly fail the job description.
  • vs sync=disabled—the tempting benchmark cheat: acknowledge sync writes as if durable. The performance of a SLOG for free, paid for with the exact guarantee the application asked for. Fine for scratch data you can regenerate; a lie anywhere durability was the point.

Why does a CoW filesystem with atomic transaction groups need a log at all? Because txgs commit every few seconds, and a sync write can't wait seconds—the contract is "durable now." The ZIL bridges the gap: a minimal record of the acknowledged operation lands on stable storage immediately, while the real write travels the normal CoW path in its txg. When the txg commits, the log records become garbage without ever being read—the happy path discards the log continuously.

So the ZIL is the deliberate exception to "everything flows through the tree": a small, forward-only side channel purchased solely so that fsync can return in device-latency time instead of txg time. Understanding that makes the SLOG sizing folklore obvious—it holds seconds of sync writes, not gigabytes of data (a few GB is plenty), and its virtue is latency, not bandwidth.
