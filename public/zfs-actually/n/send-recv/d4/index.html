Send and Receive

concept
depth 3/4 − deeper + surface
Snapshots, backups, and actually sleeping at night 5/6 ← prev next →

Send and receive are how a snapshot—a frozen moment of a dataset— travels to another machine. send turns the moment into a flowing copy; receive, on the other end, rebuilds it perfectly: every file, every setting, the snapshot itself. And after the first full trip, ZFS ships only what changed between one moment and the next.

The mental slot to avoid: this is not a file-syncing tool with extra steps. Sync tools (like rsync) hunt for differences—walking every folder, comparing files, approximating sameness against a moving target. ZFS doesn't hunt, because it already knows exactly what changed between two frozen moments. The copy isn't approximately the same; it is the same.

The shape of use:

  • Full: zfs send tank/ds@snap | ssh backup zfs recv vault/ds—a stream through a pipe, composable with ssh (this is where your SSH keys earn their keep).
  • Incremental is the daily workhorse: zfs send -i @yesterday @today ships only the blocks born between the two snapshots. Cost scales with churn, not dataset size—a 10TB dataset that changed 2GB sends 2GB.
  • Interrupted transfers resume (-t with a receive token)—relevant the first time a multi-TB initial send meets a flaky link.
  • Nobody types this on schedule; syncoid wraps the whole dance. But run it by hand once—the pipe makes the architecture legible.

The load-bearing constraint: incrementals require a common snapshot on both sides. The shared snapshot is the reference point the diff is computed against; destroy the last common one (say, by hand-pruning snapshots the replication depended on) and incremental replication is over—next send starts from zero. This single fact explains most real-world replication breakage, and it's why retention policy and replication schedule have to be designed together (sanoid's job, syncoid's job—one config).

Boundaries: send/recv moves datasets between pools; moving whole pools between machines is import/export, no copying at all. And the tape-archive temptation—piping sends into files for cold storage—is brittle (one flipped bit invalidates a whole stream, and incrementals chain); streams are transport, a live receiving pool is storage.
