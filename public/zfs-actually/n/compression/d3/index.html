The inherited model: compression trades CPU for space—a tax you pay only when the space is worth it. Actually, with LZ4 the trade usually runs backward: compressing costs less time than reading and writing the extra uncompressed bytes would, so compression frequently makes the pool faster. It early-aborts on incompressible data (media files cost you almost nothing), and it's the OpenZFS default posture—"set compression=lz4 on your pools' root datasets so that all datasets inherit it unless you have a reason not to enable it" (OpenZFS docs).

Your 90s-CPU intuition about gzip was correct then. The hardware moved; the intuition didn't.

Operating it:

  • Set once at the top (zfs set compression=lz4 tank), let inheritance carry it down. On current OpenZFS, compression=on means lz4 already.
  • Check what it's buying: zfs get compressratio tank/...—text, logs, and databases commonly land 1.5–3×; already-compressed media sits at 1.0× and cost you a rounding error to check.
  • zstd is the deliberate upgrade: better ratios than lz4 for more CPU, tunable levels (compression=zstd-3). Worth it on datasets that are compressible, written moderately, and read a lot—archives, logs. lz4 remains the right default reflex.

The temporal gotcha (same rule as every ZFS property): it applies to newly written blocks only. Flipping it on rewrites nothing—old data stays uncompressed until something rewrites it. Don't be puzzled by a 1.0× ratio on a dataset that was full before you enabled it.
