Snapshots, backups, and actually sleeping at night 3/6 ← prev next →

The lived version: you clear 100GB of artifacts to free space, run zfs list, and almost nothing came back. Forty years of filesystems said deleting frees space. Actually, deletion only frees blocks that nothing references—and every snapshot taken while those files existed still references them. The space didn't leak; it moved from the dataset's ledger to the snapshots' ledger. Deleting files in the present cannot unpin the past. (Operational source: the practitioner's own hourly-snapshot dataset doing exactly this.)

The diagnostic, in order:

  1. zfs list -o space tank/...—read USEDDS (the live dataset) vs USEDSNAP (held by snapshots). A big USEDSNAP is this exact situation.
  2. zfs list -t snapshot -o name,used,creation tank/...—see who's holding. Remember each snapshot's used shows only unique space, so a chain can collectively hold far more than the column suggests.
  3. To reclaim: destroy the snapshots that span the deletion. Dry-run first—zfs destroy -nv tank/ds@old1%old8 (a range, with -n showing what would free without doing it).

⚠ Destructive—zfs destroy of snapshots is permanent, and under a policy tool it's also usually wrong: sanoid will keep pruning on schedule anyway. Adjust the retention policy, not individual snapshots—hand-culling what a policy engine manages is how you end up with neither the space nor the history.

The two intuitions this scenario finally reconciles: "snapshots are free" (true—at creation) and "deleting frees space" (true—absent references). Snapshot cost is divergence: churn-heavy datasets (databases, build dirs, VM disks) under hourly retention accumulate held space fast; static media barely accrues any. Which means retention policy is a per-dataset decision—the databases might warrant aggressive hourlies pruned in days, the media library weekly snapshots kept for months. One-size retention is how the pool fills.

The graduating insight: USEDSNAP isn't waste—it's the storage price of your history, itemized. The question is never "how do I make it zero" but "is this history worth this rent," dataset by dataset.
